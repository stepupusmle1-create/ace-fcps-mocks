Renal · Q 14 / 50 Tutor mode ⚑ Flag

A 58-year-old man with congestive heart failure is started on furosemide. Two weeks later his serum potassium is 3.1 mEq/L and serum bicarbonate is 31 mEq/L. Inhibition of which transporter is primarily responsible for this drug's diuretic effect?

Correct answer: B71% of students answered correctly · sample question

Explanation

Furosemide is a loop diuretic. It blocks the Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ (NKCC2) cotransporter in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, where about 25% of filtered sodium is reabsorbed, which makes it the most potent class of diuretic. The extra sodium delivered to the collecting duct is exchanged for K⁺ and H⁺, causing hypokalaemia and metabolic alkalosis, exactly as in this patient.

Why the other options are wrong

  • A. The Na⁺/Cl⁻ cotransporter is blocked by thiazides (e.g. hydrochlorothiazide), which act on the distal convoluted tubule.
  • C. ENaC is blocked by amiloride and triamterene, potassium-sparing diuretics that would raise, not lower, potassium.
  • D. Carbonic anhydrase is inhibited by acetazolamide, which causes a hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis, not alkalosis.
  • E. Aquaporin-2 insertion is driven by ADH; it is antagonised by vaptans (e.g. tolvaptan), which cause free-water loss without this electrolyte picture.
Educational objective

Loop diuretics inhibit NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb. Increased distal sodium delivery causes hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis.
